The pest-control company had given me a two-hour window on a weekday afternoon. Normally that's the least interesting sentence a landlord can write. This time I read it twice, because I had reason to think the tenant would be home during those two hours. After weeks of nobody answering the door, that made it the most useful piece of information I had.

The contractor was coming for a genuine pest treatment, and that's all they were there for. But if someone was going to be in, it was also a window I could pass to the process server, the person whose job is to hand a legal notice to the tenant. So a routine maintenance booking turned into a run of calls between me, my lawyer and a process-serving company, all for the chance to deliver one piece of paper.

Let me back up.

## How I got here

By October, the rent on my Illinois rental was months behind, and there had been shortfalls before that. I'd already told the tenant I didn't intend to renew the tenancy. Now I needed help with the unpaid rent, so I hired a lawyer and paid a deposit.

What surprised me was how much homework came first. You can't just ring a lawyer and tell them your tenant owes you rent. I sent the lease, housing-related correspondence, my rent ledger, electronic payment records, earlier notices and screenshots of messages. I had to show which payments had come in and what was still outstanding.

It wasn't the whole story, either. The tenant had raised concerns about repairs and told me they were withholding rent because of them. My records showed what had and hadn't been paid. They couldn't settle whether the withholding was justified, and no court has looked at that.

My lawyer then prepared a formal five-day notice for the unpaid rent. That was a separate document from my earlier notice about not renewing. I read it, signed it and sent it back so a process server could deliver it.

One thing I didn't understand at the start: hiring an eviction lawyer doesn't mean you have a court date. My lawyer's first piece of work covered preparing the notice and sending it to a licensed process server. Taking a case to court would be a separate stage, with separate costs.

## Why one piece of paper mattered so much

If you've never been through this, you might be wondering what I wondered. If the rent's already months behind, why does it matter so much whether one notice gets delivered?

Because in Illinois, that notice is where the formal process starts. [Illinois Legal Aid Online](https://www.illinoislegalaid.org/legal-information/dealing-unpaid-rent) explains that landlords have to serve a written notice before they can file an eviction case for unpaid rent. The five days are a chance for the tenant to pay once the notice has been properly delivered. They're not a countdown to the landlord getting the property back on day six. According to the [Illinois Courts guide to filing an eviction case](https://courts.illinois.gov/forms/approved/eviction/EVC_HowtoFileandPresentanEvictionCase.pdf), the day the notice is served doesn't even count. The clock starts the day after.

If that period passes and the rent is still unpaid, the landlord can generally file a case. The tenant can turn up, dispute what's owed and raise defences. A judge decides who gets possession. And if anyone has to be physically removed under an eviction order, the sheriff does that, not the landlord. Local and housing-specific rules can change the details, so take this as the general shape rather than a timetable for every rental in the state.

I do understand why it works this way. Someone should get proper notice and a chance to be heard before they lose their home. I just hadn't appreciated that everything else would wait on getting that one piece of paper into somebody's hands.

## Weeks of asking whether it had been delivered

Weeks after I signed the notice, I was still asking my lawyer a basic question. Had anyone actually delivered it, and if so, on what date?

The service company told me it had made repeated attempts, and the period it had set aside for that round had ended. As I understood it, the company needed updated paperwork before it could try again.

That's where the pest-control appointment came in. I told the service company about the window. They said I could call again the next day and they'd see whether they could fit someone in, as long as they had the right paperwork. I asked my lawyer to get the documents over in time. Nobody promised a server would turn up, and I never got confirmation that the notice was served during that appointment.

## Out by the end of the week?

Meanwhile, the tenant was talking to me about leaving. During the same stretch, they told me they could be out by the end of the week. They asked for money to help them move, and for me to sign something saying they'd been a good tenant.

That second request sat badly with me, given the rent dispute. I wanted the property back, but I wasn't signing anything before I had the keys and written confirmation that they'd moved out. We had no agreed handover date.

It was a strange position to be in. I could talk to the tenant about their moving plans while still not knowing whether the formal notice had reached them. And I was trying to work out whether I'd have the keys back by the weekend or another bill to pay.

## Five visits, no answer

Then my lawyer sent me an affidavit of non-service. That's the process server's written, sworn record of attempts to deliver a document that didn't succeed. The server reported five visits with no answer. The mailbox appeared full, and they saw no vehicles during those visits. Those are the server's observations. I wasn't there, and nobody has established why the door went unanswered.

My lawyer raised the possibility that the tenant might already have moved out. He suggested that I, or someone acting for me, could arrange a proper inspection after giving 48 hours' written notice, and he sent me a notice-of-entry form. That's a different thing from the five-day notice. It's about entering the property to check on it, not about the rent. In a separate reply, he explained that if I wanted the process server to keep trying, I'd have to pay for another round.

An unanswered door, a full mailbox and a tenant saying they could be out by the end of the week all felt like clues. None of them told me whether the property had actually been handed back.

## Where things stand

That's where I am as I write this. I have a record of five unsuccessful visits, a suggestion for checking whether the property is occupied, and the prospect of another bill. I don't have a confirmed move-out, the keys or an eviction order.

I can open my ledger and see exactly what rent is missing. What I can't find in it is the time I've spent chasing paperwork, ringing the service company and trying to coordinate a visit around a pest-control booking. And after all of that, I still don't know whether I'll be collecting the keys or paying somebody to knock on the door again.

*I'm not a lawyer, and this is my own experience, not legal advice. If you're dealing with unpaid rent, speak to a licensed attorney in your state.*
