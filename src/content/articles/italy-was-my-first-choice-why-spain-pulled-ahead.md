"Sorry meant more Italy than Spain."

That's what I typed, almost without thinking, in one of my early conversations about moving to Europe. I'd said Spain, corrected myself, and meant it. Italy genuinely was the country I was leaning towards.

I'm British, but I've lived in the United States for the past 20 years. Gavin and I live on Long Island, and when you've been away from Europe that long, it's surprisingly hard to picture what everyday life there would actually look like. I wasn't dreaming about villas. I was trying to imagine the ordinary bits. How easy would it be to get back to the UK? Could I carry on making my Amazon videos? How would my income be taxed? Could Gavin and I go together?

Those questions turned out to matter much more than my first instinct.

## Three countries on the list

France, Spain and Italy were all on our original shortlist. We wanted warmer weather, easier trips back to the UK and the possibility of buying a home with a search budget of around €250,000.

France had a lot going for it, and when we talked it through, taxes and everyday living costs came up. There wasn't one dramatic moment where a French rule ruled it out. It's just that, as the conversations went on, the two countries I kept coming back to and comparing were Spain and Italy. France quietly slipped to the side.

## My Amazon videos changed the visa question

My first instinct was to think about a passive-income residence route. I asked whether earning around $4,000 a month from my Amazon business could support that kind of application. That figure was a scenario I wanted to explore, not something I was already earning every month, and not an official threshold for anything.

I had a reason for thinking of my commissions as passive. An Amazon storefront video I made months ago can still earn money while I'm asleep. That part really does feel passive.

But there's the other side of it. In one month alone, I made 198 Amazon storefront videos. I researched the products, filmed them, uploaded them and kept the storefront going. That isn't sitting back and waiting for money to arrive. That's work, and quite a lot of it.

So the question changed. It stopped being "is this income passive?" and became "which residence route would let me keep doing the work that earns it?"

That's what drew me towards Spain's digital nomad route, which Spain officially calls international teleworking. I started asking much more practical things. How would I document income that comes from overseas? What evidence would someone running an online business need to show? Where might my Walmart affiliate income fit, or Pintanstic, the Pinterest tool I'm building?

I want to be clear that these were questions I was investigating, not answers I've found. I haven't established that those income streams would qualify, and I know there's no monthly figure that guarantees anyone approval. But it was the first time a route seemed to match the way I actually work.

## What I meant about taxes

When I compared the two countries, my words were: "for tax purposes, Spain seems to be the better option."

I'm keeping the word "seems", because it's honest. I still had a lot to work out, and I still do.

In my research, Italy's often-mentioned 7% regime for pensioners came up again and again. So did Spain's special tax regime that people usually call the Beckham Law. Both sound appealing when you read about them in an article. But what I really wanted to know was simpler and harder at the same time: how would either country treat the money I might actually live on?

Today, that could include creator commissions or business income. Further down the line, I may draw from my American 401(k). (For readers in the UK, a 401(k) is a US retirement account, a bit like a workplace pension.)

This is where my 20 years in America matter in a very practical way. Being British tells you which passport I hold. It doesn't tell you where I've built up my retirement savings, or where my commissions come from. Both of those are in the US. So I couldn't look at an attractive tax rate in a headline and assume it answered my questions. I don't know yet whether I'd qualify for any special regime, and I'm not assuming Spain would give me a lower tax bill.

I also had to keep reminding myself that visas and taxes are two separate things. A route that lets you live somewhere doesn't decide how your income is taxed once you're there, and a good tax regime doesn't get you a visa.

## Could we go together?

Gavin and I both hold British passports, so in some ways we start in the same place. But when I looked at Italy, I kept coming across family paperwork, including a term I'd never heard before: "nulla osta".

My worry was very straightforward. Could separate application steps mean one of us was ready to move while the other was still waiting? I never established that Gavin would face any fixed delay, and I'm not saying he would. It was simply a question I couldn't shake.

With Spain, the possibility of including Gavin in the application process from the very beginning really appealed to me. I also wanted to understand his right to work once we were there.

And that's when I heard myself say it: "Spain's going to be the better option really, doesn't it."

After comparing three countries, reading about tax regimes and working through how I earn my money, one of my biggest questions came down to something very ordinary. Could the two of us pack up our home on Long Island and go together?

## The moment I knew Spain had pulled ahead

Italy still appeals to me, and I don't think that will ever completely go away.

But the clearest sign that my thinking had shifted wasn't a big decision or a spreadsheet. It was the kind of question I started asking.

At the beginning, I was correcting myself because I meant Italy. Later, I was asking what records I should keep from my Amazon business, just in case, for a possible Spanish application.

That's where I am now: still exploring, still asking questions, and quietly keeping my records in order.
