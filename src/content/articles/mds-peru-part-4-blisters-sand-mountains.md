*MDS Peru Diaries, Part 4. Photos: me and Tugce.*

In [Part 3](/stories/mds-peru-part-3-into-the-ica-desert/), Tugce and I finished stage one as the happy last two in the race, rolling into camp long after the fast runners. But the day wasn't over yet.

## Dinner in the dark

For dinner I went over to Tugce's tent and met her group: Shita from India, Yuk from Hong Kong and two men. They were all so welcoming.

Because we were the last ones back, it was already dark and most of the camp was asleep. Not us. We were trying to work out how to boil water for our freeze-dried meals, but the flame kept blowing out.

In the end we tucked the stove into an empty tent to shelter it from the wind, while quietly worrying we might burn the whole bivouac down. There were a lot of giggles from everyone as we tried to work it out. Eventually dinner was cooked and eaten, and no tents were harmed.

![Cooking by head torch in the dark](/uploads/mds-peru-img_8323.jpg)

## Feet first

Next it was time to look after my feet. I already had a few blisters. The medical team pops them and wraps you up, and nobody asks if you're going to quit. People out there aren't quitters.

Tugce had a few blisters too, and the team showed her how to look after them herself. The two of us were yelling in pain and laughing at the same time.

Sleeping in the tent was fine, and I slept OK. We were ready for day two.

![Morning in the bivouac](/uploads/mds-peru-img_8237.jpg)

## Stage two: the sand mountain

Day two was the hard day. We set off at 6:37 in the morning and didn't finish until almost 5:30 in the evening, and the big challenge was a huge climb up a sand mountain. It felt like we were climbing forever. You get to the top, and there's another mountain to climb. My watch shows we climbed from almost sea level to 691 metres in the first 13 km, all of it on sand and gravel.

![The slope disappearing into the clouds](/uploads/mds-peru-img_3333.jpg)

![The view from the top, down to the Pacific](/uploads/mds-peru-img_3597.jpg)

When we finally reached the top, there was still another section to go before the next water stop, and we'd run out of water. Thankfully, MDS has orange-jacket team members who run the course backwards to check on everyone. One of them shared his water with us, and we made it to the checkpoint.

We carried on, but my feet were in a bad way. I had the wrong footwear: my shoes were too small and my gaiters were no good. I'd trained in them on the beach near my home and they'd been perfect. Out here they were terrible.

![My shoes and gaiters, taped up](/uploads/mds-peru-img_8229.jpg)

Stage two lets you choose your distance at a checkpoint along the way. Tugce and I didn't hesitate: we chose the shortest route straight away and kept going.

## Lost in a sandstorm

Then a sandstorm blew in. The sand in the air made it hard to see where we were going, and even harder to spot the next checkpoint. Whenever we picked up the pace and started running, we'd lose the route markers, and we got lost a few times. Each time we had to stop, look around and find our way back onto the course.

## The door in the desert

As we walked, we saw a silver door standing in the middle of the dunes. Just a door, on its own, miles from anything.

Later, when we asked people about it, most of them hadn't even seen it. To this day we can't work out what it was.

![The mystery silver door in the dunes](/uploads/mds-peru-img_8282.jpg)

![Tugce heading across the dunes](/uploads/mds-peru-img_8285.jpg)

## "You're not far now"

We had to stop a few times because of my feet. Each time, a van turned up to check we were OK. Every runner wears a tracker beacon, and if you stop moving for a while, the team comes to find you. It's very reassuring.

They always tell you you're not far away, so keep going. They tell you that even when you are very far away.

We crossed the finish line after people who'd done the longer distance, but we'd had so much fun.

> **Stage two in numbers (2 December 2025)**
>
> - **Distance:** 30.40 km (the shortest route option)
> - **Time:** 10 hours 47 minutes (10:45 of it moving)
> - **Average pace:** 21:13 per km
> - **Climbing:** 1,036 m of elevation gain, almost double stage one
> - **The sand mountain:** from near sea level up to 691 m over the first 13 km, then straight back down to the coast by about 20 km
> - **Calories burned:** 2,199
> - **Bonus:** my running app still called it my 2nd fastest 30K. I'll take it.

![My stage two elevation profile, showing the sand mountain](/uploads/mds-peru-img_8519.jpg)

## The medical tent

My first stop was the medical tent. My feet were a mess, but I wasn't alone. Lots of people were in the same state. The medics had to cut away a large patch of skin from the bottom of my feet.

I was annoyed with myself for not preparing properly. I'd been so focused on my injured knee for so long that I hadn't thought enough about my shoes. But there was nothing I could do about it in the middle of the desert.

![Both feet taped up after the medical tent](/uploads/mds-peru-img_8311.jpg)

> **Lesson learned: get your race shoes right**
>
> - Train in the exact shoes and gaiters you'll race in, on the same kind of terrain.
> - Feet swell over long days in the heat, so many desert runners go up a size.
> - Soft beach sand isn't the same as hours of dunes, gravel and hard climbs.
> - Pack a good foot-care kit and learn to treat your own blisters before you go.

Lesson learned: I've since bought Salomon trail shoes made for sand, and they're ready for my next desert adventure.

Tugce came to check on me, and I got chatting to the people around me. Something I found interesting was how many of them worked in engineering, tech or ran companies. Lots of very similar jobs, and then there was me, a fashion designer. But we were all like-minded people. You have to be, to sign up for something like this.

## Rest day

The next day was a rest day: a chance to rest my feet and body and take in the views. It was very hot, so I mostly slept and chilled in the bivouac.

![Rest day: bandaged feet up at tent 28](/uploads/mds-peru-img_8319.jpg)

MDS put on a yoga session, and a van turned up with fresh fruit, which was a lovely touch. I'm not exaggerating when I say an orange in the middle of the desert was one of the best things I ate all trip. In past years they've handed out cans of Cola on rest day, and we were secretly hoping for that, but no such luck.

It was also laundry day. Zuzanna showed me how to wash our things in camp. She genuinely loves laundry, which made her the most useful person in the desert. Tugce hadn't packed spare clothes to save weight, so she ended up wearing her emergency blanket as a skirt. Desert couture.

![Tugce in her emergency-blanket skirt and me in my shoe covers](/uploads/mds-peru-img_3839.jpg)

The doctor had told me not to go in the ocean because of my bandages and how much the salt water would sting. So, of course, I went in. My feet stung. Bad move. The beach was also covered in crabs, which I hadn't planned for either.

But the views were amazing, the chatting was fun, and the sunset over the Pacific was the perfect end to the day. In the evening there was the race briefing for the final stage.

![Beach selfie with friends](/uploads/mds-peru-img_3843.jpg)

![Sunset over the Pacific from the bivouac](/uploads/mds-peru-img_3690.jpg)

One stage left, and we were all ready for it.
